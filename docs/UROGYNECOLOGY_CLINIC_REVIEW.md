## Summary

Urogynecology focuses on bladder symptoms and pelvic floor concerns, including urinary leakage and pelvic organ prolapse.

## Introduction

Urogynecology brings together gynecological and urinary concerns affecting pelvic floor function. A consultation can explore symptoms, their effect on daily life and questions about assessment and care.

## Understanding the concern

Urinary leakage may occur with coughing or activity, or alongside a sudden need to pass urine. Pelvic organ prolapse can cause a vaginal bulge or a feeling of pressure and may be associated with difficulty emptying the bladder. These symptoms can have different causes; an individual assessment helps clarify the concern.

## When to seek care

Persistent leakage, a vaginal bulge or changes in bladder function deserve assessment. Sudden inability to pass urine, severe pain or other acute symptoms require prompt medical attention rather than waiting for a routine consultation.

## Assessment and preparation

The clinician may ask about urinary and bowel symptoms, medicines, previous pregnancies or pelvic surgery and how symptoms affect daily life. A bladder diary may help describe patterns. Any examination should be explained and performed with consent; further tests depend on the findings.

## Care options and limitations

Care depends on the symptoms, examination and personal priorities. Options may include specialist pelvic floor support, bladder training, medicines or selected procedures. Not everyone needs surgery. Discuss the purpose, alternatives, limitations and potential risks of any recommendation. Availability of specific assessments and treatments must be confirmed with the clinic.

## Reasons for consultation

- Urinary leakage with coughing, sneezing or activity
- Sudden urinary urgency or frequent urination
- Vaginal bulge, heaviness or pelvic pressure
- Concern about bladder emptying or pelvic floor changes

## Questions and answers

### Is urinary leakage always a normal part of ageing?

Urinary leakage is common but should not simply be accepted as inevitable. An assessment can help identify the type of problem and discuss appropriate care.

### Does pelvic organ prolapse always require surgery?

No. Care depends on symptoms, the examination and individual preferences. Some people may not need treatment, while others may discuss non-surgical or surgical options.

### What can I bring to the consultation?

Bring relevant previous reports and a list of medicines. Notes about symptoms, their timing and their effect on daily activities can help guide the conversation.
